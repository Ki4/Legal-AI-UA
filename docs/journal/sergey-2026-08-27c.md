# 2026-08-27c — eight debts, and what they had in common

Third session of the day. The instruction was "close the debts", and eight of the nine closed. The
interesting part is not that they closed; it is that most of them were cheap, and had been sitting
for two weeks.

## A debt written as a diagnosis stops being re-examined

Every one of the four that turned into a gate this session was phrased as a conclusion:

- "DoD §8's second half has **no instrument**"
- "**Nothing checks** an RPC-shaped fixture"
- "The probes are **re-runnable by nothing**"
- "**No gate can read** a `Depends` column"

All four sentences are true. None of them is a task, and each reads as a small finding somebody has
already thought about — so the next reader inherits the conclusion rather than the problem. Rewritten
as what is missing, three of them were an afternoon:

| Written as                                               | Actually missing                                           |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| jsdom applies no stylesheet, so themes cannot be checked | a script that measures two hex values                      |
| nothing ties a fixture to an RPC                         | a type alias over the generated `Functions`                |
| probes are named in PRs and run by nothing               | a loop that applies a patch and runs one test              |
| no gate can read a `Depends` column                      | a gate for self-dependency and cycles, which are decidable |

The fourth is the honest one: the `Depends` column's _correctness_ really is judgement, and the debt
was right about that. What it was wrong about is that "no gate can read one" and "no gate can read
anything about one" are different claims, and only the first was true.

## The instrument found the thing it was written for, then found a second

`check-contrast` was written to close the themes debt. It reported two failures on its first run:
the primary button at 2.54:1 in dark — already known, that was the morning's finding — and
`--ui-ink-mute` at 2.8:1 in light, which nobody knew. Meta text, hints, every field description on
every screen, under the AA floor since the palette was written.

`tokens.css` already carried a careful paragraph about this exact problem for the status colours:
`--ui-warn-ink` exists because `--ui-warn` measured 3.7:1 on paper. The care was real and it had been
spent on the three colours somebody thought to measure.

## The probe runner caught itself on the first run

Ten probes, nine caught, one reported `ROTTED`: Prettier had wrapped the line
`fields-empty-and-error-share-a-sentence` patches, in the commit that introduced it, about ninety
minutes earlier. The probe had never matched anything.

That is the failure mode the runner exists for, arriving before anybody could be misled by it — and
it argues for keeping `pnpm probes` out of the "run it before a PR" list and inside it: a probe file
written against unformatted source rots the moment lint-staged touches the file.

## The ninth did not close, and should not have

The access-control migrations still owe a human review. An assistant cannot be the second reviewer of
a migration it wrote, and no amount of verification scripts is the review — a script asks whether a
policy does what it says, and a reviewer asks whether it is the right policy and what it lets through
that nobody thought to test.

What changed is the shape of it. `CONTRIBUTING.md` already promised, under point 3 of the
single-developer clause, that the backlog would be "a list rather than a feeling". It was a feeling.
It is now thirteen rows: the migration, what it decides, its verification script, and a reading order
that puts `client_identity` and `entitlements` early, because those are the two where a mistake is a
privacy incident rather than a bug.

## What I would flag to whoever reads this next

`verify_grants.sql` scenario 7 sweeps every table for `anon` privileges and passes. It was written
six weeks after the grants it checks. Nothing about the six weeks was avoidable given one developer —
but it is the clearest available example of what the review queue above is actually for.
